# 55 — What Small Firms Can Copy

The portable lessons are principles, not Globant's headcount, brand, or Studio names. Globant's public [services, Studios, and industries](https://www.globant.com/) make specialization and customer context legible; its filing describes long client relationships and multiple contract forms. A smaller firm can reproduce clarity and discipline without reproducing breadth.

| At Globant scale | Underlying principle | At 100 people | At 25 people |
|---|---|---|---|
| many Studios | make expertise findable | 2–4 proven practices with leaders | one sharp specialty plus honest adjacencies |
| many industries | understand buyer context | choose one or two repeatable domains | document one workflow deeply |
| major customer stories | evidence reduces uncertainty | permissioned cases with problem, role and result | one referenceable result, narrowly stated |
| account leadership | preserve relationship and commercial context | named account owner with delivery counterpart | founder or lead owns continuity |
| consulting and design offers | diagnose before prescribing | paid or bounded discovery | structured workshop and decision memo |
| architects and specialists | test feasibility before promises | rotating presales architect | senior engineer joins qualified calls |
| technology alliances | borrow platform knowledge and verification | one meaningful vendor practice | certifications only where used |
| global governance | make distributed work observable | weekly decisions, risk and outcome review | short cadence, named escalation owner |
| expansion across accounts | earn the next mandate | quarterly outcome/opportunity review | ask after value is demonstrated |

## Copy the constraint, not the costume

A branded “Studio” without deep practitioners is theater. A partner badge without delivery evidence is weak reassurance. A glossy case study that omits the firm's actual contribution creates ambiguity. The copyable behaviors are harder and cheaper: say what you know; bring technical authority into the pursuit; record assumptions; define customer responsibilities; communicate bad news early; and connect delivery measures to the buyer's desired outcome.

Discovery and communication are especially scalable. A 25-person firm can ask who uses the process, where delay occurs, what systems and controls constrain change, how value will be measured, and who decides. It need not employ a global consulting division. Conversely, small size can improve senior access, but only if leaders actually attend and decisions do not bottleneck around them.

“Land and expand” also scales down only as an ethic: deliver a bounded first result, learn the account, and qualify another problem. It is not permission to underprice entry work or manufacture dependency. Expansion remains a new buying decision.
