# 59 — The Discovery Gap

A specification describes requested behavior. Discovery investigates whether that behavior addresses a valuable problem under real constraints.

```text
“Build these 17 requirements.” → estimate requested implementation
“Our reconciliation takes four days.” → investigate work, cause, impact and options
```

The first can be legitimate: a capable customer may already have performed research and architecture. The danger is assuming completeness. The second is not yet a software scope. It requires observation and evidence.

## From statement to decision

A disciplined discovery asks:

1. **workflow:** where does reconciliation begin, wait, branch, repeat and end?
2. **people:** who operates, approves, suffers delay, funds change and owns risk?
3. **systems/data:** which sources, identities, interfaces, volumes and quality defects exist?
4. **economics:** what labor, delay, leakage, customer harm or control exposure results?
5. **constraints:** deadlines, policy, architecture, vendor, accessibility and operating windows;
6. **outcome:** what measurable behavior should change, and what must not degrade?
7. **uncertainty:** which claims need logs, samples, interviews, prototypes or spikes?

An output may be a problem statement, current-state map, evidence log, options, risk register, target measures, technical hypotheses and a next decision. It need not be a large report. Good discovery can conclude “do not build,” “change the process first,” or “buy/configure rather than custom-build.” That independence builds credibility but conflicts with compensation that rewards only engineering volume.

Globant's public [services](https://www.globant.com/services), design/consulting capabilities, and acquisitions such as [WAE](https://www.globant.com/news/globant-acquires-leading-digital-strategy-and-design-firm) support the documented conclusion that it presents capability beyond generic programming capacity. They do **not** reveal a universal proprietary discovery method. The workflow above is a general professional-services model.

A firm waiting for perfect specifications competes primarily on execution, staffing, rate, and reliability. A consulting-oriented provider competes earlier: it helps determine what should be bought. Neither is universally superior. Customer maturity, ambiguity, regulation, urgency and provider competence determine the useful model.

Discovery creates a second problem: someone must translate its business findings into engineering choices and engineering limits back into a buying decision. [Chapter 60](60-the-technical-commercial-gap.md) examines that bridge.
