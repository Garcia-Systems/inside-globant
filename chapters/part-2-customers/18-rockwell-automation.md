# 18 — Rockwell Automation: Digital Engineering for an Industrial Technology Company

Rockwell Automation sells industrial automation and digital-transformation technology. It is itself an engineering company whose products operate in production environments. Globant and Rockwell have publicly announced a relationship, and Globant presents Rockwell customer material ([Rockwell Automation newsroom](https://www.rockwellautomation.com/en-us/company/news.html), [Globant customers](https://www.globant.com/customers)). The public detail is thinner and more provider-led than the Formula 1 case, so this chapter does not fabricate a factory implementation.

## The industrial context

Industrial software differs from a consumer campaign. Production systems may have long asset lives, controlled upgrade windows, safety and availability constraints, edge connectivity, proprietary protocols, and users whose workflow cannot pause for a redesign. Rockwell also serves its own customers; work on its enterprise or product systems may affect a business-to-business ecosystem rather than a direct consumer journey.

**Documented fact:** Globant identifies Rockwell Automation as a customer and describes digital/engineering collaboration in public customer material. **Unknown:** the complete product and plant boundary, architecture, named technologies, operating responsibility, customer-side KPI, commercials, and continuous duration.

Because the surviving reliable sources do not support the substantial account reconstruction requested, the evidence limitation is itself reported. A better-documented industrial replacement was not found among the reviewed first-party Globant materials; substituting a less industrial but richer consumer case would defeat this chapter's portfolio purpose. Rockwell remains as a bounded industrial counterexample rather than padded narrative.

## Consumer brand versus industrial enterprise

| Dimension | Consumer-facing engagement | Industrial-enterprise engagement |
|---|---|---|
| primary user | shopper, viewer, fan, driver | operator, engineer, administrator, partner |
| failure visibility | abandoned journey or brand harm | workflow disruption; potentially production impact |
| system cadence | often frequent channel releases | may be constrained by assets and validated windows |
| domain knowledge | behavior, content, commerce | operations, controls, product and plant context |
| evidence sought | adoption, conversion, engagement | reliability, throughput, quality, maintainability |

These are general contrasts, not disclosed Rockwell project facts.

## What remains structurally similar

Both environments begin with a user or operating problem, translate it into a product and system boundary, assemble disciplines, integrate with an existing estate, test behavior, and measure value. Both require internal ownership and external knowledge transfer. Both punish a team that optimizes an interface while ignoring the process behind it.

An industrial customer may buy external help despite deep engineering capability for the same bounded reasons seen elsewhere: a specialty, additional throughput, experience with a platform, or a multidisciplinary team. The sources do not reveal which rationale governed Rockwell. That unknown should become a Part III interview question rather than a confident conclusion.
