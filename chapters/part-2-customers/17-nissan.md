# 17 — Nissan: Building the Journey Between Brand and Vehicle

An automotive customer journey crosses advertising, research, configuration, finance, dealers, purchase, ownership, service, and sometimes connected-vehicle software. The automaker does not control every system in that chain, yet the customer experiences one brand. Globant's Nissan case study describes digital customer-experience work and reports provider-selected results ([Globant Nissan story](https://www.globant.com/customers/nissan)).

## What can be established

**Documented fact:** Globant publicly identifies Nissan as a customer and describes a digital-experience engagement. The case is evidence of the work Globant claims and the measurements it reports. Nissan's global site and reports establish the wider automotive strategy, but public customer-side material reviewed here does not independently verify every case metric ([Nissan reports](https://www.nissan-global.com/EN/IR/LIBRARY/ANNUAL/)).

The safest reconstruction is therefore bounded:

```text
fragmented or improvable customer journey
→ experience and product capability
→ customer-specific digital surface and connections
→ provider-reported engagement/conversion improvement
```

Readers should consult the live case for the exact metric, market, period, and wording; the source does not justify projecting a result across Nissan globally.

## Why a package is insufficient

An automaker can buy content management, commerce, analytics, CRM, and cloud products. Those products do not contain Nissan's vehicle catalogue, market rules, dealer relationships, finance choices, brand expression, legacy interfaces, or governance. Services value lies in deciding how components should behave together and engineering the missing connections and experiences.

That does not mean every feature should be custom. A sound delivery may configure a platform, reuse components, and write only differentiating logic. The public story does not disclose the build-versus-buy decisions. It does support the broader lesson that a customer purchases an integrated outcome, not simply a software license.

## Industry specificity

Automotive knowledge changes the questions asked during discovery. Is a “lead” routed to a dealer? Are configurations valid in a specific market? How do model-year and inventory changes propagate? Does a journey end at a form, a reservation, or a sale recorded elsewhere? Which data may cross country or partner boundaries? These are research questions prompted by the context, not claims that Globant owned each process.

**Reasonable inference:** effective delivery needed collaboration with Nissan stakeholders who held product, market, and system knowledge. An external team cannot infer those rules solely from a design brief.

**Unknown:** exact Nissan entity and full geography beyond the case wording; technology stack; dealer-system interfaces; data architecture; team; operating responsibility; relationship origin and duration; price; and independently measured commercial effect.

## The distinct lesson

For a manufactured product, digital engineering shapes demand and ownership without manufacturing the vehicle. Its value can come from reducing friction and joining systems across organizational boundaries. That work is customer-specific enough that a boxed application alone is unlikely to complete it, while internal ownership remains essential because the rules and channel relationships belong to Nissan.

## Questions to Think About

1. Which parts of an automotive digital journey are differentiating, and which should be standardized?
2. How would you trace a web engagement metric to a vehicle sale without overstating causality?
3. Why are dealer and market boundaries as important as interface design?
4. What evidence would reveal whether this was a project or a durable account?
