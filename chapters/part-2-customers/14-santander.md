# Chapter 14 — Santander: External Engineering Inside a Technology-Intensive Bank

Santander is not a bank discovering software for the first time. Its public strategy treats technology and digital channels as core capabilities. That makes the interesting question not “why outsource IT?” but why a bank with its own technologists would add an external digital-engineering firm.

Globant publishes a Santander customer story describing work on digital experiences; Santander's corporate materials independently demonstrate the scale and strategic importance of digital banking, but do not validate every Globant case-study detail ([Globant Santander story](https://www.globant.com/customers/santander), [Santander annual reports](https://www.santander.com/en/shareholders-and-investors/financial-and-economic-information/annual-report)). The entity, geography, period, and system boundary must therefore be read exactly as stated on the case page rather than generalized to the entire Santander group.

## The customer problem outward

A bank's channel is the visible edge of a deeper system. A seemingly simple customer action can touch identity, consent, security, product rules, transaction systems, records, fraud controls, observability, and customer support. Design changes must coexist with operational resilience and regulatory obligations. Santander's annual reporting supports the broader regulated context; it does not prove that Globant owned compliance or core banking.

**Documented fact:** Globant describes customer-experience and digital work for Santander and reports outcomes on its provider-authored page. Those results should be attributed to Globant unless corroborated by Santander.

**Reasonable inference:** an external multidisciplinary team can complement—not replace—bank personnel by bringing a packaged combination of research, design, product, and engineering capacity to a bounded journey. Collaboration is especially plausible when internal owners retain business rules, risk decisions, and platform accountability.

**Unknown:** procurement route, sponsor, complete architecture, data access, security responsibilities, exact division of labor, pricing, causal attribution for results, and whether the named work led to later scope.

## What collaboration can mean

| Layer | Likely customer ownership in a bank (general) | Possible external contribution | Santander-specific proof? |
|---|---|---|---|
| product and risk decisions | customer executives and control functions | research and decision support | division unknown |
| journey and interface | product/channel owners | service design, UX, prototyping | Globant describes digital experience work |
| software delivery | internal platform owners | application engineering and testing | only to the extent stated in case material |
| regulated controls | accountable bank functions | implementation within approved controls | Globant accountability not documented |
| operation | bank and its operators/vendors | support or continuous delivery | unknown |

This model explains why “has engineers” does not imply “must build everything with employees.” The relevant comparison is marginal: can an integrated external capability reduce time, add a specialty, or increase throughput without creating unacceptable coordination and control costs? Public evidence does not reveal Santander's calculation.

## The distinct lesson

In finance, domain knowledge constrains technical work. A beautiful journey that mishandles authentication, disclosures, accessibility, auditability, or failure recovery is not successful. Industry knowledge is therefore not decorative sales language; it helps translate intent into acceptable requirements. Nevertheless, regulatory importance should not be used to claim Globant performed regulated functions that no source names.

## Questions to Think About

1. If Santander already employs technologists, what additional value must Globant provide for the relationship to make economic sense?
2. Which decisions can be delegated to a delivery partner, and which accountability should remain with the bank?
3. What evidence would establish that a digital-experience improvement caused a business result?
4. How might security and compliance requirements change discovery before any interface is built?
