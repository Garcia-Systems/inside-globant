# 13 — Electronic Arts: The Lesson in a Long Relationship

Electronic Arts (EA) makes and operates interactive entertainment. Games combine creative production with software engineering, proprietary tools, online services, releases across hardware platforms, live operations, and demand that can change around launches. That context makes specialized capacity potentially valuable—but it does not tell us what Globant did.

Globant's FY2024 Form 20-F identifies EA as a customer and describes the relationship as extending for more than a decade; Globant customer material also presents EA work ([FY2024 Form 20-F](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/browse/?CIK=1557860&owner=exclude), [Globant customer directory](https://www.globant.com/customers)). These provider sources establish public, repeated relationship evidence. They do not provide a customer-validated contract history.

## What the evidence supports

**Documented fact:** the relationship spans many years according to Globant's filing. That duration is stronger evidence of repeat purchasing than a one-time logo. Globant associates its gaming capabilities with product and engineering work, but the public account materials reviewed do not permit a complete title-by-title ledger.

**Reasonable inference:** repeated work for a games publisher requires familiarity with production pipelines, quality expectations, intellectual-property controls, and release rhythms. It also makes retained knowledge valuable. This is an operational inference from relationship duration and industry context, not a claim about EA's confidential staffing model.

**Unknown:** which EA executive first sponsored Globant; the initial problem; studios, titles, and systems covered in every phase; use of EA proprietary technology; team locations; staffing model; commercial structure; quantified outcomes; and whether acquisitions contributed to expansion.

## Specialized talent is more than language proficiency

“Game engineering” is not merely writing code with a game engine. Depending on scope, it can include tools, build pipelines, porting, online services, telemetry, testing, art production workflows, and live content. A provider valuable in this environment must combine technical disciplines with the customer's production constraints. Yet no public source reviewed supports assigning each of these activities to the EA account. They are a map of discovery questions.

Long duration changes the external-help logic. An internal team may own product direction and core intellectual property while an external team supplies bounded delivery capacity or specialties. The economic case is not necessarily “outsiders are cheaper.” It can be access to a practiced team, the ability to expand or contract, and preservation of internal focus. For EA, those explanations remain hypotheses until account-level evidence appears.

## What duration proves

Duration establishes that the relationship was not merely announced and forgotten. It does **not** prove monotonic growth, strategic-account status in every year, satisfaction with every project, exclusivity, or profitability for Globant. The cautious conclusion is narrower and useful: a digital product company can repeatedly purchase external technical capability even though engineering is central to its own business.

That pattern challenges a simplistic divide between “technology companies build” and “nontechnology companies outsource.” Sophisticated builders may still assemble mixed internal/external production systems. The value of the services provider then depends on integration with the customer's methods, not replacement of the customer's product ownership.

## Questions to Think About

1. What must an external game-development team learn beyond programming tools to become useful?
2. Why is relationship duration evidence of repeat purchase but not necessarily account growth?
3. Which responsibilities should a game publisher be reluctant to externalize, and why?
4. What technical artifacts would best reveal the boundary between EA and Globant teams?
