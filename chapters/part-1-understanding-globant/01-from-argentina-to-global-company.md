# 1 — From Argentina to Global Company

Globant was founded in Buenos Aires in 2003 by Martín Migoya, Guibert Englebienne, Martín Umaran, and Néstor Nocetti. Its own history describes an ambition to build from Latin America for global customers; its 2014 initial public offering made it the first Latin American software company to list on the NYSE, according to Globant ([company history](https://www.globant.com/about), [2014 IPO announcement](https://investors.globant.com/news-releases)).

The significant point is not national pride or a founder story. It is an early economic design: reach demand in larger overseas markets while organizing scarce digital engineering capability in Latin America. Geography could offer talent, cultural affinity, and overlapping hours with North America. It also imposed constraints—distance from buyers, a lesser-known brand, cross-border contracting, travel, and the need to understand industries and executive priorities.

## Growth as a sequence of missing capabilities

| Stage | Problem to solve | Capability required beyond coding |
|---|---|---|
| Argentina to export work | Win trust outside the home market | client access, English-language collaboration, delivery proof |
| Regional scale | Staff multiple programs reliably | recruiting, management, reusable specialist communities |
| Public company (2014) | Fund and govern a larger institution | disclosure, controls, investor access, acquisition currency |
| Multicontinent expansion | Serve clients and acquire capabilities in more markets | local relationships, integration, consistent governance |
| AI-era positioning | Move from implementation capacity toward transformation relevance | consulting, data/AI expertise, partnerships, responsible deployment |

The sequence is analytical, not a claim that Globant internally used this exact roadmap.

## Milestones that changed the machine

The company established delivery and client-facing presence beyond Argentina, grew across Latin America, North America, Europe, and later Asia-Pacific. Acquisitions were not all interchangeable headcount purchases. For example, Globant said its 2016 acquisition of US-based WAE added strategy and design capability; the 2021 acquisition of CloudShift added Salesforce expertise in the United Kingdom; and the 2022 acquisition of GeneXus added a low-code platform and ecosystem ([WAE announcement](https://www.globant.com/news/globant-acquires-leading-digital-strategy-and-design-firm), [CloudShift announcement](https://www.globant.com/news/globant-acquires-cloudshift), [GeneXus announcement](https://www.globant.com/news/globant-acquires-genexus)). These announcements establish intended strategic rationales, not proof that every integration objective was achieved.

The acquisitions illustrate three ways a services firm can expand:

* **market access**—relationships and credibility closer to buyers;
* **capability access**—specialists, methods, or platform credentials it would take time to build;
* **geographic capacity**—additional locations from which work can be delivered.

Globant's filing describes acquisitions as part of its growth strategy and also warns that integration, retention, valuation, and cultural alignment create risk ([FY2024 Form 20-F, risk factors and acquisitions](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/browse/?CIK=1557860&owner=exclude)). Growth therefore adds coordination work as well as capacity.

## What problem was Globant solving?

At first approximation: global organizations needed digital-product engineering, while Latin America contained talent that could be connected to that demand. But scale changed the problem. A larger client does not purchase a country; it purchases confidence that a provider understands the objective, can mobilize a suitable team, manage security and delivery, and remain accountable. Globant consequently needed brand, senior customer relationships, consulting and design, specialist organization, legal entities, operational controls, and partner credibility.

**Reasonable inference.** The Studio model and acquisitions can be read as answers to the trust and relevance problem: they give a customer a vocabulary for capability beyond “developers available.” **Unknown.** Public records do not disclose which early sales tactics created each anchor account or precisely how founders divided commercial and technical responsibilities.

## Access, proof, and repeatability

International growth can be understood as three successive commercial problems. **Access** means getting a credible conversation with an overseas buyer. **Proof** means showing that a distant provider can deliver something important. **Repeatability** means turning founder-led success into an institution that can win, staff, and govern many accounts at once. A reference customer can help with proof; it cannot by itself supply repeatability.

This lens makes the public listing more than a celebratory date. A listed company can be evaluated through audited reports, use equity and capital in acquisitions, and signal institutional permanence to some buyers. It also assumes recurring disclosure and control costs. Likewise, a new country is not automatically “growth.” It is valuable only if it improves customer access, capability, delivery resilience, or talent economics enough to offset another legal, managerial, and cultural boundary.

Acquisitions compress time but do not abolish integration. Buying a consultancy may provide senior relationships and methods immediately; retaining those people after the transaction and connecting them to a global delivery organization is a different achievement. The public announcement supports intent. Evidence of later combined customer work, retained leaders, or durable capability would be needed to evaluate realization. This is why the timeline separates event from interpretation.

## Questions to Think About

1. When does a location advantage become a coordination burden?
2. Why can buying a design consultancy change a provider's access to executive problems even if it adds fewer engineers than a delivery acquisition?
3. What institutional capabilities does an enterprise customer expect that a small engineering shop may not need?
4. How would public-company status alter both opportunity and operating discipline?
