# Chapter 35 — Enterprise Buying and Procurement

“We like the solution” does not mean “we have a deal.” A sponsor's preference must pass through an enterprise decision system.

Globant's filing describes written agreements, confidentiality, data/cybersecurity, legal and regulatory exposure, insurance and revenue-recognition judgments ([FY2024 Form 20-F](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/browse/?CIK=1557860&owner=exclude)). This proves that commercial infrastructure and contractual obligations matter. It does not expose a customer's procurement sequence.

## A general enterprise buying model

| Gate | Question |
|---|---|
| business sponsor/economic buyer | is the outcome important enough and funded? |
| procurement | is selection defensible and commercially competitive? |
| legal | are liability, IP, warranties, termination and disputes acceptable? |
| security/privacy | may this supplier access these systems and data? |
| risk/vendor management | is the provider stable, insured and governable? |
| architecture/technology | does the solution fit standards and dependencies? |
| executive/governance approval | does authority match commitment size and risk? |
| onboarding | can identities, purchase orders and operating controls be established? |

Actual ordering varies. This is not Globant's proprietary process or a claim that every gate is separate.

Enterprise buying is multilateral. Users may value experience; architecture may value coherence; security may restrict data; procurement may challenge price; finance may control budget; legal may reallocate risk. A technically commercial translator helps explain why a requirement changes design, cost or outcome without dismissing the control function as bureaucracy.

Large established providers invest beyond engineering because customers purchase organizational reliability. Global legal entities, security programs, finance, contracting and account governance can make a provider buyable. Scale does not guarantee approval; it supplies evidence and capacity to undergo review.

**Unknown:** bidders, evaluation weights, master agreements, negotiation history, security findings, insurance limits, approval thresholds and time-to-signature for published Globant cases.

## Questions to Think About

1. Which stakeholder can stop a deal without owning its business outcome?
2. Why should security enter before the final proposal?
3. What makes a provider technically capable but commercially unbuyable?
