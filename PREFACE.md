# Preface
## How a Global Technology Services Company Connects Business Problems with Global Engineering Talent

[Inside Globant](README.md) · [About This Book](ABOUT-THIS-BOOK.md) · [Full Contents](CONTENTS.md)

Globant is a useful case because it makes a difficult economic question visible: how does engineering capability distributed across countries become work that a major customer can understand, buy, govern, and trust? Public filings show the scale and economics of the company. Customer and technology-partner publications show selected relationships. Neither reveals the whole mechanism between an initial conversation and a delivered result.

This book studies that mechanism. It is not a corporate history, although history helps explain how Globant acquired capabilities and market reach. It is not investment analysis: financial figures are used to understand a services business, not to value its shares. It is not a sales manual: the reconstructed lifecycle is a general professional-services model, not Globant's confidential process or a promise that a particular technique will win work.

The evidence has firm boundaries. Regulatory filings are authoritative at company level but aggregate. Official service pages establish what Globant offers, not what every customer bought. Case studies are selected accounts of work and outcomes, often published by the provider. Job advertisements show that a role was sought at a particular time, not a universal organization chart. The text therefore distinguishes documented fact, reasonable inference, general industry model, and unknown. The [evidence-gap register](sources/evidence-gaps.md) makes important unknowns explicit.

The five Parts form one argument. Part I identifies the company and its operating model. Part II follows customers to determine what is actually purchased. Part III reconstructs the functions that can turn a need into a feasible engagement. Part IV adds the platforms and alliances that influence access, architecture, and implementation demand. Part V asks which functions remain necessary when the organization is much smaller—and which may be combined or supplied externally.

The central proposition is simple but not automatic: engineers create technical capacity; valuable commercial relationships require additional work that connects customer demand to reliable delivery. The chapters that follow test what public evidence can support about that connection, and stop where the evidence stops.
